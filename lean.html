Proofs a computer believes

Lean 4, Mathlib, and no benefit of the doubt

A proof assistant will not accept "clearly," "similarly," or "the other case is analogous." You either have the argument or you find out, at three in the morning, that you don't.

I am building a Lean 4 library for combinatorics on words (words, morphisms, powers, overlaps, avoidance) and using it to machine-check both the classical theorems of the field and my own research. Every definition below type-checks against Mathlib, and every claim marked machine-checked has no gaps left open behind it.

I keep at it for two reasons. It has already caught a real error: a uniform version of one of my obligations was false, and I did not know until Lean produced counterexamples. And it is the honest way to learn a field, because you cannot formalize a definition you only half understand.

Figure 2

The word, seen from above

Take the Thue–Morse word twice, once along each edge of a square, and ink the cell where the two letters disagree. Because the parity of a binary digit sum adds under exclusive or, that cell is exactly the word's own letter at position i xor j. The square is one more picture of the same sequence.

It comes out self-similar: every quadrant is the whole picture one size down. That is the doubling rule from the research page, viewed from overhead.

What is in it

Contents of the library

Module(the spine). ✓ no gaps

Definitions for finite and infinite words, concatenation, factors, morphisms and their iteration, powers, squares, overlaps, and the avoidance predicates, plus the API lemmas that make any of it usable in a real proof.

Theorem(Thue, 1912, binary). ✓ machine-checked

The Thue–Morse word is overlap-free. Formalized from the fixed-point definition through to the full theorem, with no assumptions left open.

Theorem(Thue, 1912, ternary). ✓ machine-checked

There exists an infinite square-free word over three letters. The construction and the proof that it works, both checked.

In progress(research results). ⋯ ~72% verified

The classification from Project P2 is wired end to end in Lean: the structural argument, the upper bound, and the finite base cases compile together. Four named obligations remain open and are stated as such in the source, not papered over. One earlier version of them was refuted outright by the assistant, which is the system working.

Elsewhere in Lean

Contributions upstream

The same discipline, pointed at other people's libraries. Everything here is public, and I am a contributor to each, nothing more.

Contribution (Talos, open source). ⋯ ongoing

Talos is a WebAssembly interpreter written in Lean 4, where the definitions that run a program are the same ones you reason about. There is no second specification to drift out of sync with the implementation, which is the whole point.

My patches live on the proof side: total correctness for small programs, machine-checked equivalence between an unoptimized build and an optimized build of the same function, and the memory-region disjointness algebra those proofs stand on. Reviewing against a real codebase has taught me more about stating a theorem usefully than any amount of solo work.

Lean 4·open-source contributor

Contribution (Mathlib). ⋯ in review

Two pull requests to Mathlib, extending the list API exactly where combinatorics on words kept needing it: an enumerator for the contiguous factors of a list, and decidability of periodicity for repeated lists. Both are open and under review, which is its own education in how a large library holds its standards.

Lean 4 + Mathlib·2 pull requests

Contribution (formal-conjectures). ✓ 2 merged

Formal Lean statements of open problems for formal-conjectures, Google DeepMind's open library of unproved mathematics. Two are merged (the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture, and a Green open problem on the largest product-free sets in finite groups) and two Erdős problems are in review. The work is stating a conjecture precisely enough that a machine could one day check a proof of it.
