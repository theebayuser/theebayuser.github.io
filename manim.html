Figure 0

Two circles, one curve

A point riding one rotating arm, itself riding another turning twice as fast the other way, traces a deltoid. Nothing in that sentence is hard. Watching it happen is still better than reading it, which is the entire argument for animating mathematics.

I build these in Manim: write the scene in Python, render, watch the part that does not read clearly, rewrite. It is unreasonably good at exposing the step you thought you understood.

Figure 1

A curve that never repeats

The Lorenz system: three simple rules about how three numbers push each other around. Nothing random anywhere in it. The path still never closes, never crosses itself, and never settles onto either wing for long.

x′ = σ(y − x)
y′ = x(ρ − z) − y
z′ = xy − βz

This one draws slowly on purpose. The shape is famous enough to be a poster, and the poster loses the part that matters, which is the order the pen visits things in.

How they get made

Storyboard on paper first. If I cannot draw the key frame by hand, the animation will not save it. Then the scene in Python, usually far too long on the first pass. Then cut it in half.

The rule I keep coming back to: animate the thing that moves in the proof, and hold still for everything else. Motion is the emphasis, so spending it on decoration wastes it.
