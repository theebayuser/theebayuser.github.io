Counting Words?

what has to repeat, and what never does

Write an endless sequence of letters from a small alphabet. Can you keep going without ever repeating a block back-to-back? On two letters, no. On three, yes, and Axel Thue built such a word in 1912.

My own work is on the counting side: given a repetition to avoid, how much room does a bigger alphabet actually buy you? The answers come out as exact formulas past some threshold, with a small stubborn region below it that has to be settled one case at a time.

Vocabulary

Two words worth defining

Everything on this page is built out of these. They are less intimidating than they sound.

Definition 1 (square).

A block repeated immediately after itself. Any word of the form

x x

where x is any non-empty block. The word banana contains two of them, anan and nana. A word with none anywhere inside it is called square-free, and on three letters you can keep that up forever.

Definition 2 (overlap).

Slightly more than a square: a block repeated twice, plus its first letter once more. Any word of the form

a x a x a

where a is a single letter. On two letters squares are unavoidable, but overlaps are not, which is exactly the gap Thue's theorem lives in.

Projects

Two open lines of work

Both are in preparation. I state them loosely here and in full in the manuscripts.

Manuscript I (an exact threshold formula). ⋯ manuscript in preparation

For a family of avoidance problems, I determine the exact answer for every alphabet size past a small explicit bound, together with a complete classification of the extremal configurations that achieve it. The proof pairs a structural argument with an exhaustive finite search; the search has been independently re-run from scratch by a second, deliberately different implementation, and the structural half is being verified in Lean.

complete at computer-assisted rigor· Lean verification ~72%

Manuscript II (a classification of admissible alphabets). ⋯ manuscript in preparation

A sharp characterization of which finite alphabets admit an infinite word with a demanding combination of avoidance and richness properties. The criterion turns out to be strikingly simple, which took a long certificate to establish: a bounded search over a large but finite space, with the boundary cases hunted down individually.

classification complete, under review· reproduction package written
