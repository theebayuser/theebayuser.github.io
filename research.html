§ 01 · Research

Words, counted carefully

what has to repeat, and what never does

Write an infinite sequence of letters from a small alphabet, say just a, b, c. Can you do it forever without ever repeating a block back-to-back? On two letters, no: every long enough word contains a doubled block. On three, yes, and Axel Thue built such a word in 1912.

That gap between two letters and three is the whole subject. Combinatorics on words asks which patterns are unavoidable, meaning forced to appear in any sufficiently long word, and which can be dodged forever by a clever enough construction. The questions are elementary to state and stubborn to answer, which is exactly what I like about them.

My own work sits on the counting side of this: given a kind of repetition to avoid, how much room does an alphabet actually buy you? The answers tend to be exact formulas that hold past some threshold, with a finite, messy, interesting region below it that has to be settled by exhaustive search. Getting a machine to do that search honestly, and then proving the search was complete, is half the research.

One rule, run forever

Start with a single 0. Replace every 0 by 01 and every 1 by 10, then do it again. Each line is the whole previous line rewritten, so each is twice as long as the one before, and each begins with the one before it. Press the button and watch it grow.

Keep going and the lines converge on one infinite word. It is the Thue–Morse word, and it is the same word drawn as tick marks in every rule on this site.

Vocabulary

Two words worth defining

Everything on this page is built out of these. They are less intimidating than they sound.

Definition 1 (square).

A block repeated immediately after itself. Any word of the form

x x

where x is any non-empty block. The word banana contains two of them, anan and nana. A word with none anywhere inside it is called square-free, and on three letters you can keep that up forever.

Definition 2 (overlap).

Slightly more than a square: a block repeated twice, plus its first letter once more. Any word of the form

a x a x a

where a is a single letter. On two letters squares are unavoidable, but overlaps are not, which is exactly the gap Thue's theorem lives in.

Projects

Two open lines of work

Both are in preparation. I state them loosely here and in full in the manuscripts, and I am happy to share drafts with anyone who asks.

Project P2 (an exact threshold formula). ⋯ manuscript in preparation

For a family of avoidance problems, I determine the exact answer for every alphabet size past a small explicit bound, together with a complete classification of the extremal configurations that achieve it. The proof pairs a structural argument with an exhaustive finite search; the search has been independently re-run from scratch by a second, deliberately different implementation, and the structural half is being verified in Lean.

status: complete at computer-assisted rigor· Lean verification ~72%

Project P3 (a classification of admissible alphabets). ⋯ manuscript in preparation

A sharp characterization of which finite alphabets admit an infinite word with a demanding combination of avoidance and richness properties. The criterion turns out to be strikingly simple, which took a long certificate to establish: a bounded search over a large but finite space, audited twice, with the boundary cases hunted down individually.

status: classification complete, under review· reproduction package written

How I work

Conjecture from data first. I write the brute-force search before I write anything else, look at what the small cases actually do, and only then guess the theorem. A guess that survives contact with fifty thousand cases is worth proving.

Then rebuild by hand. Search tells you what is true; it does not tell you why. The structural proof always comes second, and it usually changes what I thought the theorem was.

Then formalize, which is where the remaining lies get found. That part has its own page: § 02, proofs a computer believes.
